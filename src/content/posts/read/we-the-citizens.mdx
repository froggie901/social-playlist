One of the first American composers, I learned about was [Aaron Copland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Copland). His most famous work is the ballet and orchestral suite, [*Appalachian Spring*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_Spring). I cannot be 100% certain, but I think where I actually first heard about Copland was on an episode of American Icons by Kurt Anderson, [*WYNC Studio presents, American Icons: Appalachian Spring*](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/studio/segments/95380-appalachian-spring). The American Icons series has a ton of fantastic episodes in its archives.

But I want to talk about one of his lesser known works, [Lincoln Portait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Portrait). This work, to me, is early example of spoken word poetry. Over the years, many famous people have narrated the text selected for this orchestral work. My favorite recording was done with [Katherine Hephun and the Cincinnati Pops Orchestra](https://youtu.be/yZ_KL3L72a8). The piece was finished in October of 1942, a year into America's full time involvement in WWII. 

 I think the piece fits with this *exact* moment. Not only because of it's connection to the American president who freed the slaves, but because of how the text used reminds us that this government is owned and operated the people. 
 
 We the people cannot escape history, and we are going to be remembered in spite of ourselved (Yes, I'm stealing from Lincoln).


> Fellow-citizens, we cannot escape history. We of this Congress and this administration, will be remembered in spite of ourselves. No personal significance, or insignificance, can spare one or another of us. The fiery trial through which we pass, will light us down, in honor or dishonor, to the latest generation. We say we are for the Union. The world will not forget that we say this. We know how to save the Union. The world knows we do know how to save it. We - even we here - hold the power, and bear the responsibility. In giving freedom to the slave, we assure freedom to the free - honorable alike in what we give, and what we preserve. We shall nobly save, or meanly lose, the last best hope of earth. Other means may succeed; this could not fail. The way is plain, peaceful, generous, just - a way which, if followed, the world will forever applaud, and God must forever bless. 
>
> <footer>Abraham Lincoln, <cite>Annual Message to Congress</cite>, December 1, 1862</footer>
